ACHIEVEMENT 1 — CHITTI

Outcome: I built CHITTI (scorewithchitti.in), an academic platform for engineering
students. It's at 9,550+ users now, 1,500+ of them paying, and it has crossed ₹3L in
revenue. It started as something free for a single branch.

What made it hard: I was the entire technical team. No budget, no cofounder, and
nobody was going to pay a student for notes they could already get free in a WhatsApp
group. The build was the easy half — the hard half was earning the right to charge for it.

What I did differently: I refused to build the whole thing. I shipped a thin slice to
2,200 people first, watched which parts they actually opened, and only fixed those.
Two more rounds of that and the paid tier sold itself, because my users had already
told me what they'd pay for. Most people build the product they imagined. I built the
one the numbers pointed at.

ACHIEVEMENT 2 — BIO MED BHARAT / POSHAN

Outcome: I won 2nd place at Bio Med Bharat and ₹50,000 for Poshan, an app that flags
developmental and nutritional risk in young children.

What made it hard: I couldn't fake this one. It screens children — if the model is
wrong, or if it just throws out a scary score with no reason, a health worker can't act
on it and won't use it twice. There is no clean dataset lying around for Indian child
growth screening, so I couldn't just throw a big model at it and hope.

What I did differently: I designed around what actually exists at a routine checkup,
and I made the app show WHY it raised a flag instead of just a number. The real work was
restraint — deciding what not to build — because a tool that explains itself gets used,
and one that doesn't, doesn't.

--- VARIANT A — for Software Engineer / Solution Engineer ---

I'd own the gap between what a drone actually does in the field and what our engineers
can see back home. Right now, when a mission half-fails at a customer site, the truth is
scattered across logs, video, weather, and the one config value somebody changed — and it
takes days to piece together. That gap is the difference between a customer trusting
autonomy and ripping it out.

Week one: I'd sit with support and shadow one live deployment, then pull three real
failure cases and rebuild each one as a single reproducible test that runs on my laptop.
Then I'd write the plain-English runbook for those three. If the next incident debugs in
minutes instead of days — and every fix lands as a regression test, not a memory — that's
a system, not a lucky week.


--- VARIANT B — for Agentic AI Engineer ---

I'd own the gap between an agent that looks clever in a demo and an agent I'd actually let
near a live drone. Anyone can wire up a loop, hand it some tools, and show you one slick
run. Almost nobody can tell you the moment it quietly started making worse decisions, or
what it should refuse to do without a human watching. That's the exact thing standing
between "cool agent" and "put it on a mission over a customer site" — and it's the part
most teams skip because it's slow and unglamorous.

Week one: I'd grab five real tasks our agents are supposed to handle and run them straight
through the agent while recording everything — every tool call, every decision point, what
it retrieved, where it stalled or guessed. Then I'd turn that into a scorecard that checks
outcomes instead of vibes: did it actually finish the task, did it pick the right tool,
did it invent a value, did it need a human and fail to ask. Once that exists, I'd lay in
the safety floor on top of it — what it must never do alone, what it hands back to a
person, and what it falls back to when a model or an API is down.

If I do that right, the team can change a prompt on Tuesday and know by Tuesday night
whether they made the agents better or just different. That's the difference between
shipping agents and gambling on them.

WHAT'S THE MOST TECHNICALLY IMPRESSIVE THING YOU'VE BUILT?
(Problem / solution / stack / outcome / links)
=====================================================================

parda — a local privacy layer that sits between you and AI tools.

The problem: I was pasting real client names, emails and ID numbers into AI tools, and
every "just self-host it" answer still sent my data somewhere I didn't control.

What I built: before text leaves the machine, parda swaps real names, contacts, Indian
IDs (Aadhaar, PAN, GSTIN, UPI, IFSC) and secrets for realistic stand-ins — a different
name, a checksum-valid Aadhaar, a believable email address — and when the AI's answer
comes back, it puts the real values back, including inside tool calls and streaming
responses. The same person gets the same fake identity every time, so the model's
reasoning still holds across a conversation.

Stack: Rust for the core (speed matters — the name model runs around 10ms per paragraph),
Python for training and evaluation, an on-device GLiNER-PII ONNX model, Aho-Corasick for
my own dictionary, checksum rules for the ID formats, an encrypted vault for the mapping,
and a local proxy that plugs into the Anthropic and OpenAI SDKs. It fails closed — if the
model is missing it refuses to send rather than leaking.

Outcome: I use it daily, it streams without breaking, and it's honest about its limits.
Code: https://github.com/deadhearth01/parda

SOLUTIONS ENGINEER INTERN — RAIL OPERATOR DISCOVERY CALL
(The first 5 questions I'd ask, and why each one matters to scoping.)
=====================================================================

1) "Walk me through one inspection exactly as it happens today — who walks the track, how
often, how long it takes, what they record, and what makes them stop everything and call
maintenance."
Why: This is the baseline. Without it I don't know what I'm replacing, what "faster" or
"safer" actually means to them, or which number makes this worth buying.

2) "Tell me about the track itself — tunnels, bridges, electrified sections, dense
vegetation, dead spots with no GPS, and the worst weather you'd still want a drone flying in."
Why: This single answer decides the whole design — where docks go, which sensors, the
flight profile, and whether autonomous flight is even legal there. Get it wrong and
everything downstream is wrong.

3) "Where do inspection findings live today, and how does a drone's output land in that
system without becoming yet another inbox nobody checks?"
Why: The classic way a pilot dies is that the output never reaches the customer's real
workflow. Integration isn't a detail — it's the difference between adopted and abandoned.

4) "What would make your inspectors trust a drone's call over their own eyes — a photo, a
specific defect class, a confidence number, or a second pass?"
Why: This sets the accuracy bar and how much human review we build in. Promise a black box
that's always right and the first miss kills the program.

5) "What approvals gate this — BVLOS, night operations, railway safety rules — and who
signs them off?"
Why: This is almost always the longest pole. I'd rather find the blocker on this call than
in week six.

(My bias on the call: no yes-manning. If a customer wants something that won't survive
contact with the site, I'd rather tell them on the call and scope the version that works.)
